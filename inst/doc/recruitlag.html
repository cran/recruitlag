Introduction to recruitlag: a worked analysis, step by step

This vignette is for a reader new to the package. There is no mathematics in it. Every step is shown, and after each step you should look at what R printed and check that it matches. If you want to know why the steps work, read vignette("mathematics", package = "recruitlag") afterwards.

The question the package answers is this. A plant population reproduces (flowers, fruits, seeds) and later new seedlings appear (recruits). If the seedlings are simply the seeds from some earlier time, then a record of reproduction and a record of seedlings should look like each other, shifted in time. The package checks whether that is possible.

Step 1. Load the packages

library(recruitlag)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)

Loading a package prints nothing. dplyr is for picking rows and columns out of tables, ggplot2 for drawing.

filter() keeps only the rows of that record: 20 units times 24 periods is 480 rows. Each row has the reproduction counted on one unit at one period and the recruits (new seedlings) seen there. Recruits are NA at periods 1 to 5, which means “not scored”: the test only uses periods that have a full lag window behind them.

Step 3. Add up over units

The first test works on the whole population, so add the units together.

totals <- r |>

Step 4. Measure how “bursty” each series is

The package measures burstiness with the Gini coefficient. 0 means every census had the same count; the maximum, which is \((n - 1)/n\) on \(n\) censuses, means everything happened at one census.

rain_gini(totals$reproduction)
#> [1] 0.02493477
rain_gini(totals$recruits)
#> [1] 0.7266465

(rain_gini() ignores the NAs.) Reproduction scores very low: it is spread evenly. Recruits score high: they are concentrated in a few censuses. Note those two numbers.

Step 5. Ask the key question

Could a delay turn the smooth series into the bursty one? The answer is found with lag_ceiling(). Give it the table of totals and tell it how far back in time a delay is allowed to reach (K = 4 means five census intervals). It finds the period, reproduction and recruits columns by name.

ce <- lag_ceiling(totals, K = 4, kernels = lag_kernels(4))
ce
#> Ceiling on the concentration of expected recruitment
#>   reproductive record: 24 periods; recruits scored at 19 periods; K = 4, lag0 = 1
#>   reproduction (theorem bound) Gini 0.025   CV 0.046
#>   searched ceiling             Gini 0.027   CV 0.050   (pure delay bins 0-0, 20 kernels)
#>   observed recruitment         Gini 0.727   CV 1.960
#>   exceedance (obs / ceiling)   Gini 27.21   CV 39.30
#>   The exceedance bounds the expected series, not the counts; see ceiling_calibration() and host_lag_test().

Read the printout line by line. The line “reproduction (theorem bound)” repeats the Gini of reproduction. That number is the ceiling: no delay, of any shape, can make expected recruitment burstier than that. The line “searched ceiling” is the largest Gini any of the delays actually tried produced on the scored censuses; it is close to the bound. The line “observed recruitment” is the Gini of recruits. The line “exceedance” divides the observed Gini by the searched ceiling: observed recruitment is many times burstier than any delay could make it.

Step 6. Draw it

rain_strip(`aseasonal, gated` = ce)

The strip is a ruler from 0 to 1. The blue tick is the ceiling. The orange dot is what was observed. The dot sits far to the right of the tick, in the region no delay can reach.

Step 7. See what a delay would actually predict

Here are four possible delays. The first one is what most population models assume: seedlings appear one census after reproduction. The others spread the delay in different ways.

delays <- list(
  `one interval only` = c(1, 0, 0, 0, 0),
  `early`             = c(.7, .2, .07, .03, 0),
  `evenly spread`     = rep(.2, 5),
  `late`              = c(0, .03, .07, .2, .7))
rain_profiles(delays)

Now apply each of them to the real reproduction series and compare with the recruits that were counted:

rain_expected(ce, delays)

The bars are the seedlings counted. The lines are what each delay predicts. All four lines are nearly flat and nearly on top of one another, and none of them goes anywhere near the three tall bars. This is the picture that the numbers in Step 5 summarise.

Step 8. The careful version of the test

Step 5 compared the average that a delay predicts with the counts that were observed. Counts are noisy, and a noisy count series can look bursty even when its average is flat. The careful test takes that into account and also uses the fact that recruits were counted separately on 20 units. It works from the unit-by-period table r directly. It takes a few seconds because it simulates many count series.

set.seed(1)
ht <- host_lag_test(r, K = 4, nsim = 2000, kernels = lag_kernels(4))
ht
#> Host-level test of the lag hypothesis: 20 units, 19 censuses, 20 profiles, 2000 simulations each
#>   observed census totals: 0 silent, Gini 0.727, CV 1.960, largest 123
#>   flat reference (phi = 0.23): P(silent) 1.0000, P(Gini) 0.0000, P(CV) 0.0000
#>   most favourable profile for the lag hypothesis, by statistic:
#>     moment       silent  P = 1.0000  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       gini    P = 0.0000  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       cv      P = 0.0000  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       max     P = 0.0030  (pure delay bins 1-1)

The last lines are the verdict. For each way of measuring burstiness the test tried every delay it knows and reports the best case for the delay hypothesis: the largest probability, over all delays, of getting a record as bursty as the one observed. If even the best case is a very small number, no delay can explain the record. The full table, one row per delay, is in ht$table:

ht$table |> select(label, phi, p_silent, p_gini, p_cv) |> arrange(desc(p_gini)) |> head()
#> # A tibble: 6 × 5
#>   label                 phi p_silent p_gini  p_cv
#>   <chr>               <dbl>    <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>
#> 1 flat reference      0.234        1      0     0
#> 2 pure delay bins 0-0 0.242        1      0     0
#> 3 pure delay bins 1-1 0.247        1      0     0
#> 4 pure delay bins 2-2 0.238        1      0     0
#> 5 pure delay bins 3-3 0.238        1      0     0
#> 6 pure delay bins 4-4 0.243        1      0     0

Step 9. A check with a record where a delay is the truth

The same data set has a record in which recruits really are a delayed consequence of reproduction. Repeat Steps 2, 3, 5 and 8 on it.

r2 <- regimes |> filter(record == "aseasonal_delayed")
ce2 <- lag_ceiling(r2, K = 4, kernels = lag_kernels(4))   # a unit-by-period table is summed for you
ce2
#> Ceiling on the concentration of expected recruitment
#>   reproductive record: 24 periods; recruits scored at 19 periods; K = 4, lag0 = 1
#>   reproduction (theorem bound) Gini 0.027   CV 0.048
#>   searched ceiling             Gini 0.027   CV 0.050   (pure delay bins 3-3, 20 kernels)
#>   observed recruitment         Gini 0.156   CV 0.282
#>   exceedance (obs / ceiling)   Gini 5.75   CV 5.69
#>   The exceedance bounds the expected series, not the counts; see ceiling_calibration() and host_lag_test().
set.seed(1)
ht2 <- host_lag_test(r2, K = 4, nsim = 2000, kernels = lag_kernels(4))
ht2
#> Host-level test of the lag hypothesis: 20 units, 19 censuses, 20 profiles, 2000 simulations each
#>   observed census totals: 0 silent, Gini 0.156, CV 0.282, largest 38
#>   flat reference (phi = 1.95): P(silent) 1.0000, P(Gini) 0.3250, P(CV) 0.3705
#>   most favourable profile for the lag hypothesis, by statistic:
#>     moment       silent  P = 1.0000  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       gini    P = 0.4315  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       cv      P = 0.4820  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       max     P = 0.7080  (pure delay bins 0-0)

Two things to notice. First, in the ceiling printout the observed Gini is again above the ceiling: counts are noisier than averages, so this on its own proves nothing (that is exactly why Step 8 exists). Second, in the careful test the best-case probabilities are now ordinary numbers, not tiny ones: the careful test does not reject a delay when a delay is the truth.

Step 10. The other way round: a seasonal plant

Everything so far used a plant that reproduces steadily all year. Many plants do not: they flower in one season. Repeat Steps 2 to 5 on such a record and watch what changes.

r3 <- regimes |> filter(record == "seasonal_delayed")
totals3 <- r3 |> group_by(period) |>
  summarise(reproduction = sum(reproduction),
            recruits = if (all(is.na(recruits))) NA else sum(recruits))
totals3$reproduction
#>  [1]   42  308  933 1261  878  342   64   12    1    0    0    9   57  299  919
#> [16] 1308  895  319   52    7    0    0    0    2

Large numbers for a few periods, then almost nothing, then large again a year later. That is seasonal reproduction. Measure it:

rain_gini(totals3$reproduction)
#> [1] 0.6750995
rain_gini(totals3$recruits)
#> [1] 0.5343037

Reproduction now scores high on the burstiness scale, because it is concentrated in a season, and recruits score a little lower than reproduction. Now the ceiling:

ce3 <- lag_ceiling(totals3, K = 4, kernels = lag_kernels(4))
ce3
#> Ceiling on the concentration of expected recruitment
#>   reproductive record: 24 periods; recruits scored at 19 periods; K = 4, lag0 = 1
#>   reproduction (theorem bound) Gini 0.675   CV 1.381
#>   searched ceiling             Gini 0.707   CV 1.513   (pure delay bins 0-0, 20 kernels)
#>   observed recruitment         Gini 0.534   CV 1.002
#>   exceedance (obs / ceiling)   Gini 0.76   CV 0.66
#>   The exceedance bounds the expected series, not the counts; see ceiling_calibration() and host_lag_test().
rain_strip(`aseasonal, gated` = ce, `seasonal, true delay` = ce3)

In the strip for the seasonal plant the orange dot sits to the left of the blue tick, inside the region a delay can reach. So a delay could have produced this record, and in this case one did: this record was built with a true delay.

This is the important point. A dot to the left of the tick does not prove there was a delay. It only says the test cannot rule one out. Try the seasonal record with the gate instead:

r4 <- regimes |> filter(record == "seasonal_gated")
ce4 <- lag_ceiling(r4, K = 4, kernels = lag_kernels(4))
ce4
#> Ceiling on the concentration of expected recruitment
#>   reproductive record: 24 periods; recruits scored at 19 periods; K = 4, lag0 = 1
#>   reproduction (theorem bound) Gini 0.668   CV 1.357
#>   searched ceiling             Gini 0.702   CV 1.491   (pure delay bins 0-0, 20 kernels)
#>   observed recruitment         Gini 0.700   CV 1.793
#>   exceedance (obs / ceiling)   Gini 1.00   CV 1.20
#>   The exceedance bounds the expected series, not the counts; see ceiling_calibration() and host_lag_test().
rain_strip(`seasonal, true delay` = ce3, `seasonal, gated` = ce4)

The gated record lands almost on the tick. With a seasonal plant, the true delay and the gate look nearly the same on the ruler, because the ceiling is so high that almost anything fits under it. The careful test of Step 8 does not help much here either:

set.seed(1)
host_lag_test(r4, K = 4, nsim = 2000, kernels = lag_kernels(4))
#> Host-level test of the lag hypothesis: 20 units, 19 censuses, 20 profiles, 2000 simulations each
#>   observed census totals: 0 silent, Gini 0.700, CV 1.793, largest 90
#>   flat reference (phi = 0.25): P(silent) 1.0000, P(Gini) 0.0000, P(CV) 0.0000
#>   most favourable profile for the lag hypothesis, by statistic:
#>     moment       silent  P = 1.0000  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       gini    P = 0.8305  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       cv      P = 0.1185  (pure delay bins 0-0)
#>     moment       max     P = 0.4405  (pure delay bins 0-0)

The best-case probabilities are ordinary numbers, even though we know this record was gated. That is not a failure of the test; it is the honest answer. When reproduction is seasonal, a burst of seedlings can always be explained as “the seeds from last season”, and no census record can say otherwise.

Step 11. What the four cases teach

You have now seen all four combinations. Keep this table in mind when you read your own results.

Reproduction Recruits Dot on the ruler Careful test (Step 8)
steady all year bursts (gated) far right of the tick tiny probabilities: no delay can explain it
steady all year true delay right of the tick, but only because counts are noisy ordinary probabilities: a delay is possible
seasonal true delay left of the tick ordinary: a delay is possible
seasonal bursts (gated) near the tick ordinary: cannot tell

The test can only say “no delay can explain this” when reproduction is steady. When reproduction is seasonal, it cannot say much either way. That is the whole reason the Lepanthes orchid in the lepanthes_* data sets is a good case: it flowers every month of the year.

Step 12. Your own data

Put your census record in a table with one row per unit (host tree, plot, population) and period, and these columns: unit, period (an integer starting at 1), reproduction and recruits (with NA at periods where recruits were not scored). If your columns have other names, tell the functions with unit =, period =, reproduction = and recruits =, as the Lepanthes vignette does. Then repeat Steps 3 to 8 with your table in place of r, and read your result against the table in Step 11. If your first lagged bin should be the census itself rather than the previous census, add lag0 = 0 to lag_ceiling() and host_lag_test().
